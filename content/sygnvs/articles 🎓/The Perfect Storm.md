There are multiple forces that act in tandem with one another to facilitate the perfect environment for information of this kind to spread at the level that it currently does. The first of these factors is the so-called “echo chamber” effect, which can be likened to Plato’s Cave; imagine that spaces within social media platforms represent separate caves in which we are all confined, and in which reality is dictated by the shadows that we see on the walls. We all exist within our own caves on social media, and often without our realization of it being the case. Twitter/X is perhaps the most emblematic of the echo chamber phenomenon, where the collection of personal browsing, engagement, and post interaction analytics is conducted and fed to an algorithm which boosts the priority of content with which we are most likely to engage. For instance, a conservative user who constantly argues with posts that have liberal viewpoints will be gradually fed more fringe left-wing perspectives and takes until their engagement with that content is maximized (Levy, Razin).

The profound effect that these echo chambers can have on public opinion is exacerbated once this behavior spills out of social media and into the real world; constant engagement with radical viewpoints allows for those views to be amplified and made to appear more prevalent than they are. As such, they make their way onto mainstream publications, who then platform those fringe ideas as if they were mainstream themselves, which boosts their legitimacy. For instance, election denial prevailed with such success in 2020 that, to this day, there is a large portion of American voters and even political officials who maintain to this day that voting irregularities and widespread corruption swayed the election in favor of Joe Biden despite the fact that, to this day, there is virtually no credible information to suggest that the election was actually rigged (Wikipedia). 

This leads us to the most common form of propaganda that casual users will encounter on social media: it is a Soviet technique that is referred to as “firehosing.” The idea is that by flooding the marketplace of ideas and news coverage with a narrative, regardless of truthfulness or accuracy, an entity can sufficiently sway public opinion by virtue of the sheer volume of coverage. This technique has already done irreversible harm to the American public, which now has one side of the political aisle believing that the other wants to rush children into gender reassignment despite the fact that such procedures have psychological and therapeutic thresholds that cannot be met before the individual has turned eighteen, and the other believing that their opposition was successfully staging fake assassination attempts in order to bolster Donald Trump’s presidential campaign in 2024.

#### **User Interaction**

The radicalization pipeline has been extensively documented since around 2017; however, the echo chambers responsible for its success are only a single element of what is allowing misinformation, disinformation, and propaganda to capture the modern media landscape to the extent that it has (Rand). Another primary cause for the consistent and breakneck pace of false news proliferation is user interaction.

If the American public were composed entirely of foreign relations and domestic policy experts, the issue of false news coverage would simply solve itself; users would observe the sensationalized headlines in their feed, acknowledge that they are fake, perhaps take a few minutes to call the falsified coverage out, and keep scrolling. However, typical users cannot reasonably be held to that level of knowledge - as such, they are all susceptible to having fake news influence their perception. This is not to say that the backend systems of platforms such as Twitter, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, etc. are guiltless, as all of these platforms reward user engagement by boosting content that is getting a lot of attention. While this aids in promoting the virality of innocuous content, such as memes, comedy and sketch content, or podcasts, it also allows for sensationalized or falsified news coverage to grain traction extremely quickly and become very widely circulated before the platforms have the ability to take corrective action (Muňoz et. al).

Going back to user behavior, however, the targeted nature of social media means that engagement rates for all kinds of posts are meant to perpetually increase. Platforms are engineered in such a way that users are only given content that they are most likely to interact with - for reactionary individuals, this means that their feed is likely to be flooded by perspectives which directly oppose theirs, thus providing them confirmation for their belief that the other side is composed entirely of radicals. For those who prefer to inoculate themselves with content that reaffirms their beliefs, they will be shown nothing but news coverage and commentary which reaffirms their pre-existing beliefs and thus pushes them further into the radical end of their ideology. 

#### **The Effect on Business**

The majority of this report concerns fake news as an element of political manipulation, which is the most transparent and obvious objective of misinformation, disinformation, and propaganda. However, if we are to remove the element of propaganda from the equation, the influences of misinformation and disinformation influence many other areas of American life that are less clear to the naked eye. For instance, we have confirmation of these two phenomena on their own influencing financial markets, stock prices, and even the public relations of individual businesses. 

One of the most famous examples of stock price manipulation, as reported by Austin Moss at the University of Colorado Boulder, involves the real estate company Farmland Partners. He writes that, “in 2018, a single false report claiming 310% of the company’s previous-year earnings were fabricated caused the stock price to drop by over 40% in just one day, wiping out tens of millions in market value.” This is not to be confused with instances like the GameStop boom of 2021, where a subreddit engaged in activity meant to incur heavy losses against hedge funds which were betting against a particular stock. That was a coordinated effort by individuals who were deeply motivated to cause havoc; in the case of Farmland Partners, a single report containing misinformation was able to tank a single stock price and nearly sink the company that was targeted. PR was badly hurt in the wake of the fiasco, with investors jumping ship and leaving the door open for larger funds to swoop in and buy up as much stock as they could. 

Now, whether the false report that crippled the financial health of Farmland Partners constitutes accidental misinformation or a coordinated attempt at disinformation by financial institutions looking to buy up shares of this business is up for debate. What is indisputable, however, is that disinformation has been used over and over again to trick consumers and investors in the cryptocurrency space to invest money in coins and projects that rob their consumers blind. There are nearly infinite examples of scams of this nature, and the harsh reality is that none of them would have been possible without promotional material that is fraught with disinformation. 

While we tend to think of disinformation as a more complicated form of lying for political gain, the most important thing that we must consider is that disinformation is more broad than that in terms of its goals. Disinformation can be spread for financial gain, stock manipulation, distraction from other nefarious activity, or any number of other purposes.
