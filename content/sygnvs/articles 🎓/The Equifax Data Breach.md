### **Abstract:**

On March 7th, 2017, the Java-based web application Apache Struts publicly announced the detection and patch of the CVE-2017-5638 vulnerability which would later be exploited in what would become one of the largest consumer data breaches in history. A day later, the US-CERT (United States Computer Emergency Readiness Team) alerted Equifax to the vulnerability and recommended that the released patch be applied within 48 hours. However, due to a combination of corporate ineptitude, human error, and the trademark inertia associated with massive, slow-moving enterprises, this vulnerability was not adequately handled within the suggested timeframe. As a result, another day later, the first bits of evidence of attackers exploiting the Apache Struts vulnerability began to make themselves apparent. Equifax employees ran a litany of scans to identify any systems containing the Apache Struts vulnerability - however, a long list of expired certificates related to the software governing threat detection devices prevented those very scans from detecting the major vulnerability that would later be exploited.

The major vulnerability in question was located within the Automated Consumer Interview System (ACIS) application: the exploit involved bypassing security within Equifax’s consumer-facing customer dispute portal. Chinese attackers were able to deploy web shells (essentially web-based backdoors into Equifax’s wider network of systems) which granted them access to virtually all of the consumer data housed on Equifax’s servers. The dedicated device for monitoring ACIS traffic was but one in the aforementioned compendium of expired security certificates, meaning that Equifax’s security personnel remained oblivious to the attack for months while it was taking place - during which time, the attackers were able to access the credentials required to gain access to a total of 48 databases. From there, the attackers were able to send up to 9,000 queries on these databases, successfully locating personally identifiable information (PII) data in 265 incidents. The data in question was then redirected to the attackers’ own servers, thus completing one of the most high-profile and damning instances of cybertheft in history.

Thankfully for those interested in uncovering the root causes of this monumental infosec failure, cybersecurity institutions, legislative assemblies and committees, cybersecurity publications, news outlets, and even university programs have published independent studies on this breach. For the purposes of this study, we will keep our list of honorable mentions relatively short: this study will incorporate information from the U.S. House of Representatives Committee on Oversight and Government Reform, the U.S. Government Accountability Office’s Report to Congressional Requesters, the Bloomberg Businessweek piece contributed to by Michael Riley, Jordan Robertson, and Anita Sharpe weeks after the events of the breach were made public, and Josh Fruhlinger’s article for the Foundry subsidiary, CSO, in order to provide a concise, definitive summary of the event as well as a series of security recommendations sourced from the absolute most trusted voices in this field.

## **Why Equifax?**

  
Along with TransUnion and Experian, Equifax makes up the final third of what is coloquially referred to as the “Big Three” credit reporting agencies (CRA’s). These three companies are the largest in America that are licensed to conduct business on a national (and occasionally a transnational) level - additionally, there are an estimated 350-450 local/regional enterprises that are within the same field, although much more specialized. A CRA’s role within the American economy cannot be understated, especially in an era in which the vast majority of American consumers hold sizable balances on credit cards, mortgages, auto loans, payday/personal loans, student financing, etc. 

As such, the purpose of a CRA is to compile information on consumers’ individual debts, SSN information, driver’s license numbers, banking information, number of accounts, etc. All of this information is then assembled behind closed doors (and without the voluntary consent of the consumers in question) to create a profile and a credit score, which is then bought and sold by banks and whomever else might be in the business of lending.These profiles are then used to calculate and judge the relative risk of lending money to certain individuals.

Because CRA’s provide a vital role in the American economy, their status is virtually unquestionable - they will remain in business so long as credit scores and financial profiles remain relevant to lenders and borrowers. However, because the information that they trade is so valuable, their security must be of the absolute highest caliber in the entirety of cyberspace; this is especially the case provided that these records are created and housed digitally, as opposed to on paper.

What the exact motivations may have been for the now-infamous 2017 data breach remain unclear, as the information that was stolen has yet to surface on any deep-web information markets. However, the consensus between the sources acquired for the purpose of this study seems to be that this was an act of cyberespionage conducted by the People’s Liberation Army of China - the 2020 suit filed by the FBI which charges four members of the PLA with the theft further legitimizes the claim that this was an act of cyberwarfare. The potential for this data to be exploited by nefarious foreign adversaries is truly remarkable and makes this particular breach one of the most prominent and prolific of its kind; for instance, because the information pertaining to international consumer behavior is so vast, the ability for adversarial hackers to determine whether certain individuals are susceptible to bribery or blackmail is greatly aided by the profiles generated by a CRA. 

The true irony is that the very people who publicized the vulnerability that would lead to the greatest cybertheft in history was the security firm whose software was protecting some of the most sensitive and valuable information in the world - Apache Struts. Once the vulnerability had been discovered by Apache personnel, patch notes were released in short order along with a list of security recommendations. However, Equifax’s lax security procedure combined with the company’s general lack of cohesive relationships between management and personnel allocated a large window of time for the vulnerability to be exploited (House of Representatives).  


## **How the Breach was Carried Out**


Perhaps the most concise summary of how the Equifax breach was possible has been provided by Fruhlinger, who writes: “Like plane crashes, major infosec disasters are typically the result of multiple failures. The Equifax breach investigation highlighted a number of security lapses that allowed attackers to enter supposedly secure systems and exfiltrate terabytes of data.” The actual server that was attacked was merely used as an entryway into Equifax’s broader systems - if attackers were to send HTTP requests with Trojan-style malicious code embedded within them, then the site could be tricked into executing it, thus allowing for attackers to intrude further (CSO). 

While the initial attack was focused on one small part of Euifax’s broader network of systems, the penultimate failure on behalf of the company was to not segment their networks prior to the breach - this is a common practice in the world of infosecurity, as the distance between networks and servers would require attackers to commit to several breaches as opposed to just one. However, because virtually all of Equifax’s data was connected to one server system, attackers were able to maneuver between servers with ease after their initial breach. This highlights the level of incompetence displayed by the team at Equifax.

What exactly caused the communication breakdown between management and the cybersecurity team remains a mystery - however, the Mandiant assessment of Equifax’s systems revealed that there were multiple expired certificates that had yet to be renewed prior to the breach (GAO). Among the expired certificates was a crucial software cert that was meant to be applied to a device delegated to monitoring network traffic; essentially, Equifax was operating their own networks with a blindfold on (House of Representatives, Bloomberg). Because of this one expiration, Equifax was not only blind to the breach while it was occurring, but was unequipped to observe suspicious traffic while massive amounts of data was being exfiltrated. 

In addition to the long list of infosec failures is the newtork scans conducted by the company in the week following the patch’s release by Apache Struts; according to the GAO report, Equifax conducted scans of their network in a vain attempt to detect the vulnerability, but failed to locate the weakness in their online dispute portal. 

Attackers were able to deploy what is colloquially referred to as “web shells,” which are online backdoors that allow attackers to gain unauthorized access to credentials, data, etcetera. Prior to the full attack, there is evidence of what is perhaps a smaller group attempting the same breach; it is likely that this small team then passed on their discovery of the dispute portal vulnerability to a more sophisticated team of hackers, which were then able to carry out the exfiltration of massive amounts of consumer data over a period of 76 days. Once the ACIS portal was attacked, hackers acquired the credentials required to access the most sensitive data on their servers by hopping between networks. 

In the wake of the attack, Equifax waited months before disclosing the breach to the American public - upon finally renewing their expired security certificates, Equifax noticed the suspicious traffic in July of 2017, taking the ACIS application offline that same month. The following month, top officials at the company held a confidential meeting to discuss findings from Mandiant concerning how severe the breach was; later, in August, Mandiant was able to confirm the severity of the attack and identify the rough number of affected consumers. It was during this time that members of the executive board of the company began selling off their shares of the company, which opens all sorts of questions related to the ethical concerns of these individuals selling their stakes prior to the disclosure of the breach to the public. Once the breach was disclosed in earnest, multiple agencies of the Federal Government launched investigations into the breach - this included the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform; the House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology; the Federal Bureau of Investigation; and the United States Government Accountability office. The glaring recommendation across virtually every study of this event is that Equifax absolutely should have renewed their expired certificates (especially those which governed their detection tools), properly segmented their networks to prevent unrelated systems from being able to interact with eachother, and the proposal of more strict regulation of credit reporting agencies as well as businesses in adjacent fields to more sternly enforce compliance with best-in-class cybersecurity practices. 


## **Aftermath and Takeaways**
  

The House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform cites in its opening pages how the aggressive growth strategies employed by the then-CEO Richard Smith contributed to the company’s lack of preparedness prior to the breach. Within less than a decade, Equifax’s stake in the credit industry had more than quadrupled in size. By the time of the attack, the one firm was in posession of data on more than 820 million consumers through the ruthless acquisition of more than 91 million businesses. 

According to Smith himself during a speech at the University of Georgia: “What do we do? We manage massive amounts of very unique data. In fact, we have data on approaching one billion people. We have data on approaching 100 million companies around the world. The data assets are so large, so unique it is . . . credit data, it is financial data – we have something like $20 trillion of wealth data on individuals, so how many annuities, mutual funds, equities you own. About $20 trillion on property data, so property that you might own – what the value was when you bought it, what it’s worth today. Utility data, marketing data, I could go on and on and on – but massive amounts of data (House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform).”

The fact that Equifax was so concerned with growth, and yet seemingly unbothered by the potential for them expose the personally identifiable information of hundreds of millions of consumers speaks to a larger problem within this industry - the absolute lack of concern for the wellbeing of the consumers whose information is nonconsentually collected by Equifax and companies like it. The overhaul that would have been required by this particular enterprise in order to ensure consumer safety was unimaginably expensive, which alone explains why Equifax was so determined to drag its feet on updating its software and renewing its certificates. It is estimated that Equifax spent roughly $1.4 billion on security updates in the wake of the breach, an expenditure which the company was obviously reluctant to take on until it threatened to affect their stock prices and public image. 

There is absolutely no viable excuse for Equifax’s ignorance to the potential for intruders to attempt am attack on their networks - this is especially the case given that Experian, another CRA, was the victim of multiple breaches just 4 years prior (CSO).

It is unacceptable, therefore, that financial index provider MSCI rated Equifax’s security efforts with a 0/10 for two years straight; MSCI’s two reports both concluded: “Equifax’s data security and privacy measures have proved insufficient in mitigating data breach events. The company’s credit reporting business faces a high risk of data theft and associated reputational consequences…(House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform).”
  

## **Reflection**

  
Throughout the undertaking of this study, a few objects of particular interest reach out to me, personally; not the least of which being that the importance of cybersecurity cannot be understated in the internet age. The protection of consumers whose only crime is accessing the internet is a calling of the most noble nature, and the study of this breach has made this obvious; between hostile foreign parties attempting mass espionage and criminal organizations looking to sell out innocent people for profit, the importance of safety is absolute. The prospect of honing my security skills in order to provide that crucial protection to people is unbelievably attractive, and the potential for me to develop my skills further into the fields of offensive security or digital forensics to hunt down these bad actors in the cybercrime and cybertheft space is, to me, the ultimate calling.

The events at Equifax have proven to me that, despite the immense risk carried by these massive organizations, they are either unwilling or unable to provide security to their customers. This is unacceptable, and if it takes offensive measures to prove to them that they are vulnerable then so be it - the ultimate objective for me going forward is to make the public safe from hackers. My hope is to develop an offensive toolset that would allow me to detect vulnerabilities on behalf of the public and report them to the companies that host them. 

From my currently ignorant perspective, even I remember the massive iCloud leak that happened while I was still in high school, during which thousands of X-rated images of celebrities were leaked - the very notion that individuals who are engaged in consensual activity are potentially subject to blackmail, extortion, or even having that material made available to the public is concerning on the deepest level. Innocent people should be protected at absolutely all costs, regardless of the potential expenditure incurred by the companies who carelessly “safeguard” their information. The amount of friends who have had social media profiles, Canvas/Slack accounts, Gmail and Yahoo! Mail accounts, and even employment portals hacked proves even further to me that this is a field that must be taken as seriously as possible, as any vulnerability can be exploited and can put people at risk who aren’t responsible for the carelessness of the organizations who host their information on their servers. 

Speaking from personal experience, my own employer has multiple vulnerabilities in their own Oracle application that would allow for any employee with management credentials to access the job applications of anyone in the company. These applications contain everything from SSN’s to phone numbers, email addresses, past employers, medical history, residential information, and even insurance information. The fact that this issue not only exists, but is so obvious that in-store personnel make constant jokes about how lax our security is as it relates to this caliber of personally identifiable information is enough to call into question whether our employer genuinely cares about the very people they claim to have such vested interest in.
