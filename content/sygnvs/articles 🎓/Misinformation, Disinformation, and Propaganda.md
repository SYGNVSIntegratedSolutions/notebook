 In the security practice, the particularly technically-inclined among us tend to hyper-fixate on hard data, confidential information, and living in terminal environments. At the end of the day, cybersecurity professionals who manage social media platforms must understand that news, by its very nature, is information and therefore data. As such, security professionals in the news or social media professions need to understand that this data is subject to the CIA Triad. Mis/disinformation, under this framework, must be scrutinized for its confidentiality (instances where reporters come into the possession of classified information, as was the case when Snowden turned over classified documents stolen from the NSA), integrity, and availability (Guardian 2). 

Integrity of information is compromised when truthful reporting is twisted in such a manner that public perception of events is distorted by mistruth, be it on purpose or by accident. 

While misinformation and disinformation are similar concepts, their actual origins and outcomes vary wildly at the extremes. Misinformation is just the publishing of information that is false or misleading; most of the time, misinformation boils down to accidental misreporting by random members of the public (Muñoz et. al). Regardless of intent, the telephone-effect of information slowly losing its integrity the further down the line it travels is a phenomenon which online platforms must take into account before boosting 3rd or 4th hand reporting. Spaces such as Twitter/X or Facebook are breeding grounds for misinformation by their very nature; individuals who are not journalists and who do not have to be concerned with journalistic integrity are as enabled as mainstream publications to reach an audience of the same scope. 

This is, however, a double-edged sword. On the one hand, this means that there is no future in which political bias in terms of news coverage can exist so long as the platforms remain unmoderated (within reason, and so long as those platforms aren’t in the business of issuing ideologically-based or of the motivated censorship of political viewpoints, which we already have evidence of being the case)(The New Yorker). However, the uglier hand reveals that the same mechanism which gives independent reporters, citizens, or just users of a given platform an equally effective metaphorical megaphone to share their beliefs and news gives bad actors the perfect vector to aid them in the transmission of falsehoods.

The mention of bad actors knowingly spreading false information leads us to our second term requiring a definition: disinformation, which performs more or less the same function in practice even though its origination is a far cry from traditional misinformation. 

While misinformation becomes amplified after erroneous reporting gets picked up by social media trends and algorithms, disinformation is associated more strongly with an intentional attempt to deceive (Muñoz et. al). This means that an actor with political motivations is putting false information into public spaces in order to sway public opinion in one direction or another.

This manifests on platforms such as YouTube or TikTok as “clickbait,” where mundane news is exaggerated in order to drive traffic towards a video with a thumbnail and video title with eye-catching imagery or wording; the content of the video can still be reporting ordinary news accurately, but studies have shown that simply having the title of the video say something outrageous can color a reader’s view of the content that they have just consumed, regardless of whether the content of the video contradicts the messaging of the title (Heydari et. al).

When dealing with the differences between misinformation and disinformation, it is worth mentioning their key similarity as it relates to traditional cybersecurity: their combined effect on the availability and accessibility of legitimate news, the final pillar of the CIA triad. The mechanisms that flood our digital town squares with misinformation rely almost exclusively on volume; the amount of noise generated by isolated pockets of the internet can easily drown out the relative quiet that legitimate coverage receives. As such, users of these platforms are more likely to be shown convincing information that is completely false than actual, reputable journalism (Vosoushi et al).

Lastly, we will explore the oldest form of manipulation and, historically, the most successful in terms of changing the political or social viewpoints of citizens: propaganda. Misinformation and disinformation typically start with a single motivated user posting information that is false or misleading, intentionally or unintentionally. 

Propaganda, on the other hand, involves a concerted effort by multiple parties that are either consciously or inadvertently coordinating to push a particular narrative. However, unlike the domestic propaganda of the 1900’s, propaganda can now penetrate international borders with the capabilities and power of the internet and promote state-sponsored messaging within targeted nations. Perhaps the most effective example of this phenomenon would be during 2016, where Russian actors were posing as a group called “Heart of Texas” and posting text, images, and videos of Hillary Clinton and Donald Trump while casting Trump as a “good” option and Clinton as a “bad” option for president. These posts had a reprehensible level of truth associated with them, and ultimately contributed to what later became known as the “Russia-Gate” scandal (University of Tennessee at Knoxville). 

## *The Big Difference*

The key difference between propaganda and disinformation is the level of coordination and the party (or parties) responsible for the transmission of such material. Disinformation can be generated by just about anyone and spread due to the nature of virality as it relates to online news coverage; a single anonymous poster can generate misleading or false news stories that gain their legitimacy as they gain traction (Vosoushi et al). The Springfield case proves this to be true, as does the infamous conspiracy theory that we know now as “Pizzagate.” 

Propaganda, on the other hand, is mostly in the business of manipulating legitimate coverage to satisfy an extreme perspective. Perhaps the most salient example of state-sponsored propaganda would be American coverage of the war in Vietnam during the conflict; field reporters observed the successes of the Vietnamese Tet Offensive, while military journalists and official government documentation labelled the strike as a failure (Britannica). 

Contemporary propaganda can include the scandal in 2024 which involved conservative podcasters and influencers accepting bribes from Russia Today to spread particular narratives amongst Republican and right-wing audiences to (Nikolaeva et. al). What makes this kind of propaganda more insidious is the degree to which hearing your favorite podcaster recite it without your knowledge influences your susceptibility to it.
