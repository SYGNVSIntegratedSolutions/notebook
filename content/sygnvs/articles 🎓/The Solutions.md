To adequately solve the problems that have been outlined, we must first address the proverbial elephant: the wide array of sources through which bogus news stories originate.

As badly as we may want to eliminate misinformation, there is simply no practical way to stop accidental misreporting or individual users misremembering news items. However, because most of the fervor around the immediately fast spread of misinformation is fueled by boosting algorithms which reward engagement, we are left with a few options that give platforms the ability to filter what content is actually enabled to meet that huge number of eyes. For instance, restricting news coverage boosting, possibly through a process that is comparable to a SIEM, to trusted outlets immediately jumps out as a solution; this would prevent individual posters from being able to gain traction with misreported news, allowing only verified journalists, news publications, and other institutions which actually have some level of credibility to reach millions of users. This solution doesn’t impede on our First Amendment rights, but also halts misinformation from being able to spread as quickly as it currently does. 

Disinformation, however, is a trickier item to tackle. While misinformation is limited to a relatively small scope, intentional disinformation can be spread by news outlets, podcasters, etc. YouTube does already have a good system in place for moderating mediums such as podcasts, however their analytics are also much more in-the-open. Manual review of a few popular podcasts and episodes, for instance, is easier than manual review of thousands of 140-word posts on platforms such as Twitter or multi-paragraph essays published on Facebook. Because platforms that reward short-form content are harder to moderate, this leaves us with only a few options. 

For disinformation that is spread by anonymous accounts, we need to see sufficient ban waves introduced to discourage this kind of activity. Keyword filtering can help, although we run the risk of blocking legitimate content based on an algorithm - a more effective system could potentially involve a process that allows users to post, but allows for platforms to attach corrective information to the post. For instance, we can look at Twitter’s community notes system - the only downside to this system is that the notes are driven by popular consensus as opposed to objective truth. Instead of outsourcing this responsibility to the public, this process should be moved to a moderation team that is within Twitter’s ability to control. 

Because the community notes system relies on the public, they are able to contribute to disinformation instead of fighting against it - switching to a system that allows for internal generation of notes allows for the platform to mandate that notes contain information that is objectively true. While the turnaround for a system like this is objectively slower, it would have a more tangible impact; people would be more inclined to take notes of this nature seriously if there was any faith in the system. One additional measure that can be tacked onto this process would be using keywords to follow reposts of the same disinformation and immediately tag them, or by hunting disinformation actors by utilizing a massive user analytics database; while the logs would be frighteningly long and tedious to sort through, such a system would protect the integrity of legitimate news by periodically removing bad actors that threaten the availability of true news stories by flooding the market with nonsense. 

The final solution for the disinformation problem that will be proposed here would be the combination of multiple robust systems to produce the ultimate disinformation blocker: authentication. One platform which has conspicuously been absent from this paper thus far, despite its infamous history for spam, is LinkedIn. This is because spam and disinformation are much harder to produce in mass quantities on that platform today. While there are many reasons for this, the most effective solutions that have been implemented by that team are strict enforcement of identity verification, multi factor authentication, and spam protection. If a spammer were to attempt a mass-messaging campaign for any particular reason, their account might last a few days before being ID-banned. This means that the individual, not the user, is banned from making an account - this would force them to either produce a heap of fake licenses or IDs to resubmit verification after each ban or compromise user accounts using external means. Both processes would seriously slow down their operation to the point of not being worth pursuing; spam relies on volume.

The fact that Twitter in particular (especially with the widespread adoption of the new verified system, which is very easy to penetrate) has not implemented any kind of ID verification or user authentication is mind-blowing given the prevalence with which fake news published by fake journalists spreads on their platform.

Lastly, we can discuss potential solutions for propaganda. Because our own government is engages in propaganda, it would be a bad precedent to set if we start blocking government social media accounts (especially in the wake of the 2016-2020 Trump administration, where we get a lot of news and public announcements delivered to us through social media platforms). However, we absolutely can and should do something about foreign propaganda coming into American spaces. 

The most obvious first step is the complete prevention of posts coming in from countries that are hostile to the US. However, even this measure may be too extreme, as it gives those nations fuel to accuse the US of censorship. Instead, a centralized and constantly updated database of known propagandists and disinformation actors, the checking and updating of which is mandated by the government to enforce compliance, would be a solid compromise. This not only shows that the government is now involved in curbing the spread of foreign propaganda as a matter of national defense but shows that the corporations which own these platforms are being forced into taking preventative action on their own. This database can then be used to update ban logs and bring the most amount of labor to this issue as possible, increasing speed of deployment.

Keeping a separate database updated with logs of the stories that propagandists are looking to spread adds another layer of security to the process, as individual stories can then be moderated on the same level as accounts and sources. This process would work for disinformation as well, as the spread is usually exacerbated by multiple accounts jumping onto the same stories that are attempting to push a narrative and increase the perception of the story being true through repetition (Munoz et al). This way, stories that have the same factual errors, agenda, or radicalizing impact can be removed from the conversation.
